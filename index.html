01 — School management system

Dasmariñas North NHS
Website & Portal

Full-stack — frontend, database design and authentication

Live site

The problem

The school needed two things from one system. Outside the login: a public site that worked on a phone, carrying enrolment dates, campus information and class shifts. Inside it: a way to move a grade from the Excel class record a teacher already keeps, past the head teacher of that subject and the class adviser, and out to the learner — with the office able to see, at any point, which classes had actually handed anything in, and which learners were sliding toward failing before the term was over.

What I built

  • Three portals, five jobs — learner, teacher and office portals behind one PHP session login. Head teacher and class adviser are duties inside the teacher portal rather than extra accounts, so a teacher who also heads a department and advises a section signs in once.
  • A three-step grade review — subject teacher, then the head teacher of that department, then the class adviser, and only the adviser publishes. Heads see each sheet's share below 75 before passing it on, and holding or returning a sheet needs a comment. Published grades are locked; a correction is a full re-upload that goes through both reviews again, with the old and new marks kept.
  • Reads the teachers' own class records — teachers upload the division's E-Class Record workbook as it is. The browser reads each learner's Initial and Term Grade from the right term sheet, MAPEH halves included, and flags names that don't match the class list instead of guessing. I checked it against more than 500 of the school's real files.
  • Grading under DepEd Order 015, s. 2026 — three terms, whole-number finals against the 75 passing line, descriptors from Advancing to Emerging, the Academic Excellence Award (a 90+ average with no final below 80), Summer Remedial and promotion remarks. Advisers print report cards to PDF or Word.
  • SARDO, an at-risk prediction — a Random Forest I wrote from scratch in PHP, because the free host runs nothing else. It flags learners likely to fall below 75 next term from six predictors (below 75, a falling grade, failing three or more, frequent absences, over-age, gender) and shows why for each one. It learns from the public UCI Student Performance dataset until the office switches it to the school's own grades, and its results match scikit-learn's exactly.
  • Made for a school office to run — nothing is deleted (teachers, learners and sections are archived and can be restored), duplicates are refused, passwords only ever go out by email, every action lands in an activity log, and a one-click .sql backup restores cleanly on shared hosting. Text size and dark mode work in every portal.

What was hard

Twice, the live site broke in a way the portal should have fixed by itself. It upgrades its own database: every request checks a schema version and runs whatever changes are missing. But once the version matched, that check returned straight away — so it healed a database that was behind and did nothing for one that was damaged. When a table went missing from a database already marked current, nothing ever created it again. Once it was the table that records sign-in attempts, and nobody could log in. Another time it was the password-reset table, which broke both the office's Reset password button and the emailed Forgot link — two symptoms, one cause. The fix was a repair step that runs on every request, before the version check: logs and token tables are rebuilt empty, while the tables holding the school's actual work are never recreated behind anyone's back — the office is told which one is missing instead. What I took from it is that an upgrade path that only moves forward assumes the database is never damaged, and a live database eventually is.

02 — Mobile web app

Lean Bulk Tracker

Solo — React and TypeScript, installable on a phone; visual style adapted from a fitness-app concept

Try it live

The problem

Most calorie apps are built for losing weight. On a lean bulk the question is the opposite — how much more to eat, mostly protein, without putting on fat — and the answer moves every day you train. They also hand you one burn figure from a formula and never check it against what your scale says, and their food lists rarely know what adobo or pandesal is.

What I built

  • A target that follows the day — daily-life burn from Mifflin-St Jeor (or Katch-McArdle when body fat is known), plus the workouts you log, priced with MET values from the Compendium of Physical Activities, plus a +300 kcal surplus. Protein is 2 g/kg, fat 25%, and carbs take the rest, so they rise on training days.
  • Meal plans that hit the numbers — the app knows 222 foods, 128 of them Filipino dishes and staples alongside the usual gym foods. For each meal it picks from the ones that suit it, then solves the portions to land on the day's protein, carbs and fat in servings a person would actually eat. Swap any food and the rest re-balance.
  • "Eat next" — ranks foods by how closely their protein/carb/fat mix matches what's left of the day, with a portion that fits the calories left.
  • Measured burn — four weeks of food logs against the weight trend, a straight-line fit through the weigh-ins with a 95% margin, measure what your body really burns. It offers to correct the formula by the difference.
  • Any food, offline — Open Food Facts search for packaged foods, custom foods, and recipes built from ingredients. It installs on Android and iPhone, works offline, and keeps everything on the phone in IndexedDB: no account, no server, with export and import for backups.
  • Tested maths — 59 Vitest unit tests cover the calculations, the meal-plan generator and the food-search parsing, including 20 simulated bulking days from 50 to 100 kg that each plan must land within 5% of.

What was hard

Getting a meal plan to land on the target with portions anyone would eat. Solved exactly, the maths happily serves odd amounts like 137 g of egg; rounding each food to a real serving afterwards knocks the day off target again. So the solver works in three passes. First a least-squares fit, where each food stays inside its own minimum and maximum and errors are weighed in calories, with protein counting extra because it matters most on a bulk. Then every portion snaps to its serving step — whole eggs, half-cups of rice, 5 g of oil. Then it nudges one food at a time by one step for as long as that brings the day closer. Each meal is solved to its share of the day first and the whole day balanced last, so no meal ends up as a token portion. A big bulk can outrun sensible portions; when that happens it adds a food for whichever macro is furthest short. The random food picks are seeded, so the same day always gives the same plan.

03 — Group project

Catering Booking
Website

Team of students — my part: front-end and database design

The problem

A catering business taking bookings by phone and message has no reliable record of what was ordered for which event, and customers can't see what's on offer without asking for it first.

What the team built

  • A public catalogue of catering menus and packages with pricing.
  • Date and time selection for the customer's occasion.
  • A booking request form capturing the event details.
  • An admin side for managing menus and packages, viewing bookings, and seeing booking and sales summaries.

Bookings were requests rather than purchases — there was no online payment, and the caterer arranged that directly.

What I did

  • Front-end — the menu and package pages, the booking form, and the responsive layout.
  • Database design — the MySQL tables for menus, packages and bookings, and the relationships between them.
